# TIM RULES 2

## SAVE THE SCHOOL

*A sequel to Tim Rules*

## Chapter 1: The Quietest Monday Ever

Monday

For exactly three weeks, my life was normal.

No secret rooms.

No stolen trophies.

No mysterious messages.

No teachers running down corridors looking terrified.

Just school.

Maths.

Homework.

Lunch.

More maths.

It was horrible.

Okay, maybe not horrible. But after you have helped solve a five-year-old Black Hawks mystery and found the Festival Cup with a rubber chicken, normal school feels a little too normal.

On Monday morning, I walked through the front doors and waited for something strange to happen.

Nothing did.

A Year Seven dropped his books.

A teacher told everyone to take off their hats.

Somebody's locker made a fart noise when it opened.

That was the most exciting thing all morning.

Noah met me outside the student council room.

"You're doing it again," he said.

"Doing what?"

"Staring at the walls like they're going to open."

"I wasn't staring at the walls."

"You tried to push the trophy cabinet."

"I thought it moved."

"It's screwed to the floor."

"That's exactly what a secret door would want me to think."

Noah shook his head.

Sophie was already inside the council room. She had organised our pens by colour, put three new folders on the desk, and stuck a large sheet of paper to the wall.

It said:

A NORMAL, SUCCESSFUL TERM

Underneath that, she had written:

NO MYSTERIES. NO CHAOS. NO CHICKENS IN MEETINGS.

Brad sat beside the last rule with Frank on his knee.

Greg was on my chair.

"That last rule seems targeted," I said.

"It is," Sophie replied.

I moved Greg and sat down.

Being student council leader had become much easier since the festival. People knew who I was. Teachers actually listened during meetings. The principal had given us a small budget for clubs and equipment.

I had even started to believe I knew what I was doing.

This was my first mistake.

"We need a project for this term," Sophie said.

"We could have another pizza Friday," I suggested.

"No."

"A longer lunch?"

"No."

"A shorter Monday?"

"Tim."

"Just checking."

Brad leaned back.

"We could build a chicken arena."

"No," Sophie said.

Noah raised his hand.

"What if we make homework optional?"

Sophie put down her pen.

"Does anyone have an idea that is actually possible?"

We went silent.

That was when the intercom crackled.

"Will Tim please come to the principal's office?"

Everyone looked at me.

I looked at Sophie.

She looked at her sheet of paper.

The one that said NO MYSTERIES.

"It's probably nothing," I said.

Noah nodded.

"That's what you said before Room 75."

THE PRINCIPAL'S ENVELOPE

The principal's office looked different.

The broken lock had been replaced. The old filing cabinet was gone. There was a new photograph on the wall showing everyone at the festival.

I was in the middle, holding Greg above my head.

The principal was at the edge, smiling nervously.

Some things had not changed.

"You wanted to see me?" I asked.

He held up a brown envelope.

"This arrived this morning."

My heart did a small jump.

A brown envelope is never just a brown envelope.

Sometimes it contains a test you forgot about.

Sometimes it contains a bill.

And sometimes it contains a clue that makes you spend two weeks underground.

The principal opened it.

Inside was a single sheet of paper.

He handed it to me.

At the top were the words:

CITY SCHOOLS REVIEW

Underneath, someone had typed:

Following recent concerns regarding finances, security, building safety, and student governance, Brookfield School will undergo a full review.

I read it again.

"Is this because of Mr. Harper?"

"Partly," the principal said.

"But we fixed the accounts."

"Yes."

"We found the cup."

"Yes."

"We stopped someone stealing festival money."

"Tim, I know what we did."

"Then why are we being reviewed?"

He sighed.

"Because the people at the city office only read reports. They do not see students chasing criminals through festivals or finding missing cups. They see a school with five years of confusing accounts, damaged buildings, and a head teacher who has written 'unusual student activity' in too many incident forms."

"That last one does sound bad when you say it."

"It sounded bad when I wrote it."

He took the paper back.

"The review begins next Monday. At the end of the term, the city will decide whether Brookfield should remain open in its current form."

I frowned.

"What does 'in its current form' mean?"

"It means they may merge us with Westbridge School."

For a second, I couldn't speak.

Westbridge was our biggest rival.

Their football team beat us almost every year.

Their science fair projects always had lights.

Their student council wore matching jackets.

And their leader, Lucas Stone, had once sent us a good-luck card before a tournament and then beaten us by thirty-two points.

"They can't merge us with Westbridge," I said.

"They can."

"What happens to our clubs?"

"I don't know."

"Our festival?"

"I don't know."

"Room 75?"

The principal gave me a look.

"That is not my biggest concern."

"But it's in the top ten, right?"

He put the envelope on his desk.

"The review is not a mystery for you to solve. It is a serious administrative process."

I nodded.

"Of course."

"There will be no investigating city officials."

"Understood."

"No entering offices without permission."

"Yes."

"No forming secret teams."

"Definitely not."

He narrowed his eyes.

"Why did you say that one so quickly?"

THE FIRST MEETING ABOUT THE END OF EVERYTHING

I called an emergency council meeting at lunch.

Sophie brought the folders.

Noah brought sandwiches.

Brad brought Frank.

I brought the bad news.

When I finished explaining, nobody spoke.

Then Noah raised his sandwich.

"So... if we fail the review, do we all have to wear Westbridge uniforms?"

"That is your first question?" Sophie asked.

"It's an important question."

Brad folded his arms.

"I'm not wearing their colours."

"We are not failing," I said.

Sophie looked at the review letter.

"We don't even know what the review measures."

"Then we find out."

"Tim, the principal told you not to investigate."

"He told me not to investigate city officials. He didn't say anything about reading the rules."

Sophie stared at me.

"I hate that you sometimes use logic."

I stood up.

"We have one term. We fix everything we can. We make the council better. We make the clubs better. We show them that Brookfield is worth keeping."

Noah lifted his sandwich again.

"And pizza Fridays?"

"If it helps."

Sophie pulled the large sheet of paper off the wall.

She turned it over and wrote:

SAVE BROOKFIELD

Underneath, she made four columns:

BUILDINGS. MONEY. CLUBS. STUDENT VOICE.

I pointed at the last one.

"That's us."

"They're all us," she said.

For the first time since the festival, I felt the familiar buzz in my stomach.

The feeling that something big was starting.

Then Greg pecked the review letter.

"Greg has identified the enemy," Noah said.

We laughed.

But when I looked at the words CITY SCHOOLS REVIEW again, I didn't feel like laughing.

We had saved one festival.

Now we had to save the whole school.

THE NOTE IN MY LOCKER

After school, I found a folded piece of paper in my locker.

Nobody saw me find it.

Nobody slipped it through the gap while I watched.

It was simply there, waiting under my maths book.

I almost threw it away.

After last term, paper in unexpected places usually meant trouble.

But I opened it.

The message was written in neat black ink.

ONE CUP WAS FOUND.

ONE SCHOOL REMAINS.

ASK THE FIRST HAWK WHAT WAS NEVER RETURNED.

Below the words was a small drawing of a hawk.

Not the old Black Hawks symbol.

This bird had its wings folded.

And underneath its feet was the number nine.

I read the message three times.

Then Noah appeared beside me.

"Normal term going well?" he asked.

I handed him the note.

He read it.

"Ah."

"Exactly."

"Does Sophie know?"

"Not yet."

"Does the principal know?"

"Not yet."

"Are we going to follow Rule 2 and not investigate alone?"

I folded the note.

"Definitely."

"Good."

I put it into my bag.

"First, we find Daniel."

Noah grinned.

"There it is."

"What?"

"The face you make when normal school is over."

I closed my locker.

The quietest Monday ever had lasted until 3:17 in the afternoon.

Which, for me, was probably a record.

## Chapter 2: The First Hawk

Tuesday

Daniel Cross did not look like a former secret society member.

He looked like a man who owned too many sandwiches.

Noah and I found him at the community cafe after school. He was sitting at a corner table with a notebook, a cup of tea, and three unopened packets of biscuits.

"Emergency biscuits," he explained.

"Why three?" Noah asked.

"Because you came with him."

Daniel smiled at me.

I didn't.

The note was in my pocket, and its number nine seemed to be getting heavier.

Daniel had helped us understand the old Black Hawks. Five years earlier, he and six other students had tried to protect the school from Mr. Harper's plans. Their story had been hidden in photographs, tapes, old accounts, and Room 75.

If anyone knew what had never been returned, it was him.

I put the note on the table.

Daniel's smile disappeared.

He didn't touch it.

"Where did you get this?"

"My locker."

"Did anyone see who put it there?"

"No."

Noah reached for a biscuit.

Daniel moved the packet away.

"Those are emergency biscuits."

"This is an emergency," Noah said.

Daniel let him take one.

Then he read the note again.

"Ask the first hawk," he said quietly.

"That's you, right?" I asked.

"Yes."

"What wasn't returned?"

Daniel looked toward the window.

For a moment, I thought he was going to give us one of those long mysterious answers that sounds clever but explains absolutely nothing.

Instead, he opened his notebook.

"The Festival Cup was not the only thing we protected."

Noah stopped chewing.

I leaned forward.

Daniel turned to a blank page and drew a small rectangle.

"When we realised Harper was changing the accounts, we made copies. Emily kept the records. I kept the photographs. Another student kept the master list."

"What list?"

"Everything the school owned that had historical value. Cups. Banners. Old uniforms. The first festival programme. Documents from when Brookfield opened."

"Like an inventory?"

"More than that. It included letters from students and teachers. Promises about what the school was supposed to be."

Noah looked disappointed.

"So not treasure."

"Not everything valuable is treasure," Daniel said.

"That sounds like something a person says when there isn't treasure."

Daniel ignored him.

"We called it the Founders' Register."

I pointed to the note.

"And it wasn't returned?"

"No. After Harper discovered what we were doing, we hid things separately. The cup went missing. The accounts were scattered. The register disappeared."

"Who had it last?"

Daniel closed his notebook.

"Clara Bell."

THE EIGHTH NAME

I knew six of the old Black Hawks' names by then.

I had seen them in photographs and heard Daniel talk about them.

But Clara Bell was new.

"She wasn't on the list," I said.

"No."

"Was she the seventh Black Hawk?"

Daniel shook his head.

"She helped us, but she was never officially part of the group."

Noah brushed biscuit crumbs off his shirt.

"Secret clubs need better record keeping."

"We were fourteen," Daniel said.

"So are we. Sophie has a spreadsheet."

"Of course she does."

I pulled the note toward me.

"Where is Clara now?"

"That's the difficult part. She moved away after school. I haven't spoken to her in years."

"Did she take the register?"

"I don't think so. She cared about Brookfield more than anyone."

"Then why does the note say it was never returned?"

Daniel finally picked it up.

He studied the folded-wing hawk.

"Because this isn't about an object."

Noah sighed.

"Here comes the mysterious answer."

Daniel tapped the number nine.

"The Black Hawks had seven members. Emily wrote six rules for our group. The seventh was never written down."

"What was it?"

"Leave the school better than you found it."

I waited.

"That's seven," I said. "This says nine."

"Yes."

"Did Clara add two rules?"

"Clara believed the school charter had nine promises. She used to say the first eight were for the city, but the ninth was for the students."

The cafe door opened and a cold wind moved across the table.

Daniel lowered his voice.

"If someone is using that symbol, they know about Clara."

"Can you find her?"

"I can try."

I took out my phone.

"Tell us what you need."

Daniel gave me a serious look.

"You have a school review starting next week. You should focus on that."

"Saving the register could help the review."

"Or it could distract you from it."

"Why can't it do both?"

Noah nodded.

"Tim is very good at doing two dangerous things at once."

Daniel almost smiled.

Then he tore a page from his notebook and wrote an address.

"Clara's mother used to run the Brookfield second-hand bookshop. It closed years ago, but Clara still owns the building. Start there in daylight. Take Sophie. Do not enter if the building is locked."

Noah and I both nodded.

Daniel pointed at us.

"I mean actually locked. Not 'the door is stuck, so Brad climbs through a window' locked."

"That happened one time," I said.

"It hasn't happened to me yet. Please keep it that way."

SOPHIE'S CONDITIONS

Sophie agreed to come immediately.

Then she made us agree to six conditions.

No breaking in.

No lying to teachers.

No going alone.

No touching old electrical equipment.

No bringing rubber chickens.

No missing the council's review preparation meeting.

"That is a lot of conditions for one bookshop," Noah said.

"Those are general life conditions," she replied.

We met Brad outside school and explained everything.

He looked at Daniel's address.

"So tomorrow, we're going to an abandoned bookshop to find a missing book that might save the school?"

"Yes," I said.

"And I can't climb through a window?"

"No."

He thought about it.

"Can I bring Frank?"

"No," Sophie said.

Brad sighed.

"This club has changed."

THE CITY LETTER

Before I went home, the principal called me back into his office.

This time, Sophie was invited too.

A second letter had arrived.

The review would be led by a city schools director named Adrian Vane.

There was a photograph attached.

He had silver hair, a dark suit, and the expression of someone who had never lost a game of anything.

Under his photograph was a short statement.

Brookfield's future will be assessed according to measurable standards, not sentimental attachments.

Sophie read the line twice.

"He's talking about the festival."

"And the cup," I said.

"And everything else we did."

The principal nodded.

"Mr. Vane has already proposed a merger plan. Westbridge would become the main campus. Some Brookfield buildings would be sold."

I felt my face get hot.

"He decided before visiting?"

"He says it is only a proposal."

Sophie put the letter down.

"Then we'll give him evidence that changes his mind."

The principal looked at both of us.

"Good. Do it through the review. No shortcuts."

We promised.

Walking out, I thought about the Founders' Register.

Clara Bell.

The ninth promise.

And Adrian Vane's sentence about sentimental attachments.

For the first time, I wondered whether the review and the mystery were really two separate problems.

Or whether someone had started both at exactly the same time.

## Chapter 3: The Bookshop With No Books

Wednesday

The old bookshop still had books in the window.

That was the first disappointing thing.

I had expected an empty building with cobwebs, broken floorboards, and at least one owl.

Instead, BELL'S BOOKS was painted in faded gold letters above a clean window. Inside the glass were three dictionaries, a plant, and a sign that said OPEN BY APPOINTMENT.

Sophie checked the address.

"Daniel said it closed years ago."

"It did close," a voice behind us said. "People simply refused to stop needing books."

We turned around.

A woman with short grey hair stood on the pavement carrying two shopping bags. She looked at us, then at Brad, who was wearing his sports kit and holding Frank under one arm.

Sophie stared at the chicken.

"You brought him?"

"He was already in my bag," Brad said.

"How was he already in your bag?"

"Long story."

The woman smiled.

"You must be Daniel's students."

"You know Daniel?" I asked.

"I know everyone who ever owed me a library fine."

She unlocked the door.

"I'm Clara Bell."

THE BACK ROOM

The shop was tiny.

Shelves covered every wall. Books were stacked under tables and between old display stands. It smelled like dust, tea, and the cardboard boxes my grandmother kept in her garage.

Clara led us into a back room.

There were no books there.

Only school photographs.

Hundreds of them.

Brookfield sports teams.

Festival stalls.

Teachers with strange hairstyles.

Students standing in front of the same front doors I walked through every morning.

Clara put her shopping bags down.

"Daniel called last night. He told me about the note."

"Do you have the Founders' Register?" I asked.

"No."

"Do you know where it is?"

"No."

Noah looked around.

"Is this where you tell us a mysterious answer?"

Clara laughed.

"No. I prefer useful answers."

She took a cardboard folder from a shelf.

Inside was a photocopy of an old document.

BROOKFIELD SCHOOL — OPENING CHARTER

There were eight numbered promises.

Safe classrooms.

A library.

Fair access to clubs.

A yearly festival.

Student representatives.

Care for school property.

Honest accounts.

A school that served its neighbourhood.

At the bottom, someone had typed a ninth line and then crossed it out so heavily that the words could not be read.

"There it is," I said.

Clara nodded.

"The ninth promise."

"Why was it removed?"

"Officially? It was repetitive. Unofficially? It made a promise the city didn't want to keep."

Sophie studied the page.

"What did it say?"

Clara looked at the crossed-out line.

"Brookfield shall not be closed, merged, or relocated while its students can demonstrate that the school still serves its community."

Nobody spoke.

Even Frank stopped moving.

I read it again.

"That's exactly what the review is about."

"Yes," Clara said.

"If we have the original charter with that promise—"

"It may not legally stop the merger," Sophie interrupted. "But it proves the school was founded with student consultation."

Clara smiled at her.

"You must be Sophie."

"Why?"

"Daniel said you would be the one to mention evidence instead of treasure."

Noah raised his hand.

"He said I would mention treasure."

Clara laughed again.

THE MISSING ORIGINAL

The photocopy was useless without the original.

Clara explained that the Founders' Register contained the original charter, letters, and records of student promises from the first year Brookfield opened.

She had hidden it shortly before leaving school.

"Where?" I asked.

"In the place where every student promise was made."

We waited.

She waited too.

Noah leaned toward me.

"She said she didn't do mysterious answers."

Clara heard him.

"I said I preferred useful answers. You haven't asked the useful question."

Sophie thought for a second.

"What happened on the day you hid it?"

Clara's expression changed.

"Harper was searching lockers. Daniel had already moved the cup. Emily was copying accounts. I had the register in my bag. I needed somewhere nobody would think to search."

"Room 75?" Brad asked.

"No. Everyone in our group knew about Room 75. I needed a place outside our plan."

She opened the cardboard folder again and removed a festival programme.

On the back was a handwritten list.

PROMISE DAY

1. Library reading

2. Council speeches

3. Festival stalls

4. Trophy presentation

5. Tree planting

"A tree?" I said.

"The founders planted a tree for every new school year. Students wrote promises and read them beside it."

Brookfield still had one enormous oak tree behind the sports field.

We played there at lunch.

Brad had once tried to climb it and got stuck.

Noah had eaten lunch beneath it almost every day.

"You hid a book under a tree?" he asked.

"Not under it," Clara said. "Near it. Inside the old promise box."

"There is no promise box."

"There was. A metal box attached to a stone marker. The marker may still be there."

I put the programme into my bag.

"We'll look after school."

Clara held up one finger.

"One more thing."

Of course.

"When I left the register, I sealed the box. Only a member of the current student council could open it without damaging the lock."

Brad frowned.

"How would a lock know?"

"It doesn't. It has the old council badge shape. You need the badge."

I reached into my pocket and pulled out my STUDENT COUNCIL LEADER badge.

Clara shook her head.

"Not that one. The original."

THE BADGE IN THE CUP

The original council badge had been missing for years.

But I knew something that had also been missing for years.

"The Festival Cup," I said.

Clara smiled.

"Daniel told me you were quick."

We called him from the shop.

He answered on the second ring.

"Did you find her?"

"Yes. Did you ever look underneath the base of the Festival Cup?"

There was a pause.

"No."

"Why not?"

"Because I was more focused on not getting caught."

Fair enough.

The cup was locked in the school display cabinet. We needed permission, a screwdriver, and probably six forms signed by Sophie.

Before we left, Clara stopped me.

"Tim."

"Yes?"

"The note you received. The folded-wing hawk wasn't our symbol."

"We know."

"It was mine."

I looked at her.

"Then who sent it?"

"That's what worries me. I only ever drew it on one thing."

She pointed to the photocopied charter.

"The cover of the Founders' Register."

Someone had seen the register.

Recently enough to draw the symbol.

And they wanted me to find it.

UNDER THE OAK

At 3:30, we stood behind the sports field with a garden trowel, the festival programme, and absolutely no official permission to dig.

Sophie checked the school's site plan on her phone.

"The stone marker should be here."

There was grass.

Lots of grass.

Noah turned in a circle.

"Maybe the tree ate it."

Brad pushed Frank into my arms.

"Hold him. I'll find it."

He walked to the oak and began tapping the ground with a stick.

I looked at Sophie.

"Are we investigating?"

"Yes."

"Without a secret name?"

"We can discuss names later."

Brad stopped near a patch of longer grass.

"Here."

Under the grass was a flat stone.

We brushed away mud and found nine small lines carved around its edge.

In the centre were the words:

WHAT WE PROMISE, WE BECOME.

Attached to the side was a metal box green with age.

Its lock was shaped like a tiny shield.

Exactly like the original council badge.

Noah grinned.

"Okay. That is pretty good treasure."

Sophie looked back toward the school.

"We need the cup."

I handed Frank to Noah.

"And we need to hurry."

Because across the field, a man in a dark suit was standing beside the principal.

Even from far away, I recognised the silver hair.

Adrian Vane had arrived five days early.

And he was watching us.

## Chapter 4: Mr. Vane Arrives Early

Thursday

Mr. Vane did not like rubber chickens.

I learned this before he learned my name.

He was standing in the entrance hall on Thursday morning when Brad's bag started squeaking.

The sound came from inside it.

Squeak.

Pause.

Squeak.

Squeak.

Every student nearby turned around.

Brad kept walking.

Mr. Vane raised one eyebrow.

"Is there an animal in your bag?"

"No," Brad said.

Frank squeaked again.

Brad looked down.

"A very small one."

The principal appeared from his office.

"Mr. Vane, this is Brad, one of our student representatives."

Mr. Vane looked at Brad's bag.

"And is the chicken also a representative?"

"Frank is more of an adviser," I said.

Sophie kicked my shoe.

Mr. Vane turned to me.

"Tim, I presume."

"Yes."

He held out his hand.

I shook it.

His grip was firm, quick, and about as warm as the metal box under the oak.

"I have read a great deal about you."

"Good things?"

"Detailed things."

Noah appeared beside me.

"That's usually bad."

Mr. Vane's gaze moved to him.

"Noah."

Noah stopped smiling.

"He really has read the files."

The principal led us toward the hall.

Mr. Vane had asked to address the school before lessons.

Five minutes later, hundreds of students sat watching him straighten a sheet of paper on the lectern.

He didn't need a microphone.

His voice filled the room anyway.

THE PROPOSAL

"Brookfield has a proud history," he began.

That sounded nice.

It wasn't.

People who are about to change something always start by saying its history is proud.

"The city must decide how best to use its school buildings. Westbridge offers modern classrooms, improved sports facilities, and a larger student body. A merger could provide many advantages."

Students began whispering.

Mr. Vane continued.

"Over the coming weeks, I will assess academic performance, attendance, finances, safety, and student leadership. I expect full cooperation."

A hand went up near the front.

It belonged to Mia, one of the students who had helped during the festival.

"What happens to our clubs?"

"That would depend on demand."

"Our festival?"

"Traditions can be reviewed."

"Our council?"

"Westbridge already has an effective council."

More whispering.

Mr. Vane smiled slightly.

"Change can feel uncomfortable. But sentiment should not prevent improvement."

I felt Sophie tense beside me.

He wasn't here to discover whether Brookfield worked.

He was here to prove that Westbridge was better.

After assembly, students surrounded me in the corridor.

"Can he close the school?"

"Are we moving?"

"Do we get new lockers?"

"Will lunch be earlier?"

I answered none of the questions correctly because I didn't know.

Then Lucas Stone appeared.

LUCAS STONE

The Westbridge council leader wore a navy jacket with a silver badge.

Of course he did.

He was tall, neat, and smiling like he had already won something.

"Tim."

"Lucas."

"I heard about the review."

"You seem upset."

He laughed.

"I didn't write the proposal."

"But you knew about it."

"My council received a summary. We're preparing to welcome Brookfield students if the merger happens."

Sophie folded her arms.

"And if it doesn't?"

Lucas kept smiling.

"Then we'll see you at the City Schools Showcase."

Mr. Vane stepped beside him.

"An excellent point. The showcase takes place at the end of term. Each school presents its student leadership, community work, and plans for improvement. I have added it to the review criteria."

Lucas nodded.

"Westbridge has won three years in a row."

I looked at his silver badge.

"There's always a first time to lose."

Noah made a quiet noise that might have been a laugh or a cough.

Lucas leaned closer.

"This isn't a talent show, Tim. Weird noises won't be enough."

Then he walked away.

Brad cracked his knuckles.

"I can put Frank in his jacket."

"No," Sophie said.

"You didn't even let me finish."

"You were going to put Frank in his jacket."

She knew him too well.

THE CUP'S BASE

We had more important things than Lucas.

At lunch, the principal unlocked the display cabinet.

Mr. Vane stood nearby, reviewing papers.

He noticed us immediately.

"Why are you removing that trophy?"

"Student research," Sophie said.

That was technically true.

The principal placed the Festival Cup on his desk.

Daniel met us there. As an alumni volunteer, he could help without making the visit look suspicious.

At least, that was the plan.

Mr. Vane watched everything.

Daniel turned the cup over.

A small panel was screwed into the base.

Noah handed him a screwdriver.

"Please be a secret compartment," he whispered.

The screws turned slowly.

One.

Two.

Three.

Four.

The panel came loose.

Inside was a folded piece of cloth.

Daniel opened it.

A tarnished shield-shaped badge rested in the middle.

STUDENT COUNCIL — BROOKFIELD

Noah punched the air.

Mr. Vane looked up.

"What is that?"

"The original council badge," the principal said.

Daniel handed it to me.

The metal was cold and heavy.

Mr. Vane walked over.

"Why was it hidden inside a cup?"

"To keep it safe," I said.

"From whom?"

The room became quiet.

The principal answered for me.

"That is part of an old school matter. It has been resolved."

Mr. Vane looked at me.

"Many old matters seem to follow your council."

He wrote something in his notebook.

I wished I could see it.

Then he changed the subject.

"The review panel will visit your next council meeting. Please prepare a complete record of your current projects."

Sophie nodded.

I didn't.

Because at that moment I was thinking about the metal box, the charter, and the fact that Mr. Vane had arrived one day after the note appeared.

DIGGING IN PUBLIC

After school, we returned to the oak tree.

This time the principal came with us.

So did Clara.

Unfortunately, so did Mr. Vane.

"I am assessing student activity," he said.

"We're gardening," Noah replied.

He looked at the muddy trowel in Noah's hand.

"With a council badge?"

Nobody answered.

## Chapter 6: The Principal's Second Secret

Noah sat down.

Then Daniel started laughing.

Emily joined.

Clara laughed so hard she had to take off her glasses.

Even Mr. Vane smiled.

I did not laugh.

Okay, I laughed a little.

Daniel stood beside Noah.

Emily looked at him.

"You were terrible."

"I was enthusiastic."

"Evidence should not smell like biscuits."

"Secrets feel clever when you are scared. They feel necessary when you think nobody will listen. But they make other people carry choices they never agreed to."

He looked at Noah.

"Confession is a good start."

Noah nodded.

"And lunch," I added.

"Yes. Lunch."

THE REUNION PHOTOGRAPH

We recreated the Black Hawks photograph beside the oak tree.

Daniel, Emily, and Clara stood in front.

Justice and her mother stood with them.

The current council stood behind.

Lucas joined even though he was Westbridge.

Mr. Vane took the photograph.

Then someone called for him to join.

He refused.

Sophie set the camera timer.

The principal pulled him into the group.

At the last second, Brad held up Frank.

I held up Greg.

The shutter clicked.

Years later, people would look at that photograph and wonder why everyone was laughing.

They would not know about the forged election list, the emergency biscuits, or Mr. Vane trying to escape.

That was enough.

Helen arrived after the photograph.

No city badge.

No document bag.

No anonymous account.

Some students moved away from her.

Sophie stayed beside me.

Helen put the box down.

"I came to return these and apologise."

Clara accepted the books.

Helen turned to Sophie.

"I forged your signature. I made people doubt you. I'm sorry."

Sophie didn't answer immediately.

"Are you sorry because it went wrong or because you did it?"

Sophie nodded.

"Then you can help with the archive inventory. Publicly. With another person checking your work."

Helen agreed.

Noah leaned toward me.

"Sophie is nicer than I am."

"True."

NOAH'S LUNCH ACCOUNT

Noah paid his lunch debt in six instalments.

Wednesday: soup he did not spill on my notebook.

Thursday: pizza, because some traditions deserved respect.

Friday: a printed receipt labelled ELECTION-NOMINATION COMPENSATION.

Noah said the debt had taught him an important lesson about consent.

I said it had taught him that my lunch cost more than he expected.

He agreed both things were true.

We catalogued every document.

The register stayed in Justice's legal custody until the investigation finished.

The copied charter went on display.

The ballot copies were scanned.

Mr. Vane's altered report appeared beside the original.

Helen's forged signature sheet appeared with her statement.

Missing forms.

Poor receipts.

Unapproved investigations.

"Especially not perfect."

Students used it for history projects.

Parents added photographs.

The kitchen staff donated old menus.

The caretaker donated building maps.

For the first time, Brookfield's history did not depend on one hidden book.

It belonged to everyone.

On Friday afternoon, the archive scanner produced an extra page.

Nobody had placed it on the glass.

In the corner was a message.

ONE DRAWER REMAINS CLOSED.

No signature.

No hawk.

Just the words.

I looked at Sophie.

She looked at Noah.

Noah raised both hands.

"Not me."

I picked up the page.

Then I turned it over.

On the back was a printer error code.

The map had been stuck in the scanner's memory from an old scan.

The message was part of a historical worksheet Daniel made years ago.

Not a new clue.

Noah looked disappointed.

"So we don't investigate?"

"No."

"Not even one drawer?"

"No."

Sophie put the page in the archive.

For once, a mystery could stay old.

I had a school to run.

And Noah owed me lunch.

## Chapter 33: The Big Rematch

Saturday

The Brookfield–Westbridge basketball rematch had nothing to do with saving the school.

That was why everyone cared so much.

No lawyers.

No ballots.

No city review.

Just two teams, one court, and Brad's extremely serious face.

Lucas had organised a joint warm-up.

Both councils sold food.

Money went to the sports fund.

Mr. Vane volunteered as scorekeeper.

Noah asked whether he could review the score instead.

Mr. Vane gave him a whistle.

Noah immediately blew it.

Everyone jumped.

"That," Mr. Vane said, "is why you do not get a whistle."

Westbridge scored first.

Then again.

Brookfield missed three shots.

Brad shouted instructions.

Our players listened for about two minutes.

Then everyone tried to score individually.

By halftime, Westbridge led by twelve.

Brad sat on the bench with a towel over his head.

I sat beside him.

"What's wrong?"

"It doesn't."

Fair.

I expected a speech about partnership.

Lucas grinned.

"There he is."

Some friendships are built on trust.

Some are built on trying to destroy each other at sports.

Brookfield stopped playing as individuals.

Brad passed more.

Mia defended.

Ethan scored twice.

Noah shouted advice that had nothing to do with basketball.

We scored.

Lucas called a timeout.

Sophie held the fundraising total.

Winston watched from Lucas's bag.

Westbridge passed.

Brad intercepted.

Two defenders blocked him.

Brookfield won by two.

The gym erupted.

Brad lifted Mia onto his shoulders.

Noah ran onto the court.

Mr. Vane blew the whistle at him.

Nobody cared.

Then Brad stepped back.

"Westbridge gets it first."

"You won."

"So?"

Noah suggested a chicken battle.

Sophie suggested sharing it for six months each.

Mr. Vane suggested recording the arrangement in writing.

Everyone groaned.

We shared it.

Six months at Brookfield.

Six at Westbridge.

The first inter-school trophy that was not a cup someone had to hide.

THE REAL VICTORY

After the game, both teams practised together.

Westbridge players taught shooting drills.

Brookfield players taught defensive formations.

Brad trained harder than anyone.

He wore his Brookfield shirt under his Westbridge development-squad jacket.

It looked ridiculous.

It also looked exactly right.

The parent had no answer.

Lucas heard.

"You're getting good at speeches."

"Don't tell Tim."

"I already did."

Brad threw a basketball at me.

I caught it badly.

Some things had not improved.

THE TEAM MEAL

After the game, both teams ate in the Brookfield dining hall.

The kitchen staff made pasta for forty players and somehow produced extra when Westbridge's substitutes arrived.

Brad sat between the two captains.

For the first ten minutes, everyone discussed the final shot.

Then the conversation moved to training schedules, homework, and whether Mr. Vane had ever played basketball.

Noah asked what happened.

"I discovered that height was not the same as coordination."

Brad said that was the most honest scouting report he had ever heard.

The coaches exchanged practice plans. Brookfield would share its defensive drills; Westbridge would host shooting sessions. Nobody surrendered a team, a badge, or a trophy.

At the end of the meal, Mia returned the ball she had used to score the winning basket.

Brad told her to keep it.

"Why?"

"So you remember to pass next time."

She threw it back to him.

He caught it. I didn't. The ball hit my plate.

Lucas said Brookfield's leadership training still needed work.

THE PHOTOGRAPH

Someone took a photograph of both teams after the final drill.

Lucas stood beside him.

Mia held the game ball.

Noah held two cups and Frank.

Lucas said it was not poetic.

Brad said it was accurate.

Mr. Vane approved the correction and then complained that he had blinked in the photograph.

Noah said some historical sources could not be improved.

THE NEW SCOREBOARD

The sports fund bought a small electronic scoreboard.

Before installation, Mr. Vane checked the receipt.

Sophie checked the warranty.

Noah checked whether it could display TIM RULES.

It could.

TIM 75 — NOAH 75.

"Better."

It wasn't.

It was just a scoreboard.

Not everything had to be a mystery.

## Chapter 34: The Repairs Begin

Monday

Repairing a school is louder than saving one.

Drills started at seven.

Workmen moved desks.

Dust covered everything.

The red supports in the hall were replaced by larger yellow supports.

Noah asked why the colours changed.

Dr. Rahman said they were different load ratings.

Noah said yellow meant caution and red meant mystery.

Dr. Rahman ignored him.

Classes moved into the library and dining hall.

The council shared Room 14 with chess club.

Every meeting included background arguments about whether a bishop could move backwards.

It could.

Noah still objected.

THE NOISE COMPLAINT

Students began complaining about the drilling.

Teachers complained about the dust.

Parents complained about parking.

The workmen complained that students kept touching barriers.

I had asked for repairs.

Now everyone wanted us to stop repairing things so loudly.

Sophie put the complaints on the community-council agenda.

I held a meeting with Dr. Rahman, the caretaker, student representatives, and the contractor.

The solution was not secret.

No drilling during tests.

Quiet work during reading lessons.

Daily dust cleaning.

A pedestrian path through the side gate.

Public repair schedules.

Complaints dropped by half.

The other half were about maths.

We could not fix maths.

THE MISSING MONEY

The repair budget included new lights for the hall.

The city finance officer removed them to save money.

The drama club protested.

Without proper lights, performances would be difficult.

The sports department wanted the money spent on a new floor instead.

The library wanted roof insulation.

Everyone had a priority again.

Sophie proposed a public options meeting.

Mr. Vane warned us that too much consultation could delay the work.

He was right.

Endless meetings could become another way of avoiding decisions.

We set a deadline.

One week for evidence.

One meeting.

One vote.

No secret extensions.

The clubs presented costs.

The contractors explained safety requirements.

Students voted for the floor and insulation.

The drama lights came last.

The drama club accepted the result but asked to fundraise separately.

Fair.

Not every loss was sabotage.

Not every disagreement was a plot.

Sometimes democracy meant your favourite thing did not happen.

THE LIGHTS WE ALREADY HAD

While clearing the storage room, Ethan found twelve stage lights.

They had been bought for the festival two years earlier and never installed.

The receipts were in the archive.

The warranty had expired, but an electrician confirmed they worked.

No fundraising needed.

No new expense.

The drama club got its lights.

Noah held one up.

"So the real treasure was the equipment we forgot we owned."

"Yes," I said.

"Can we put that on the charter?"

"No."

The lights were installed after the floor and insulation.

Every club got something.

Nobody got everything.

The public budget explained why.

Mr. Vane called it a satisfactory process.

Sophie framed the comment.

Not literally.

But she wanted to.

THE HOLE IN THE FLOOR

On Thursday, a workman found a metal plate beneath the old stage.

Everyone looked at me.

I did not touch it.

I did not even suggest it might contain treasure.

Dr. Rahman checked the building plans.

It was an access cover for electrical cables.

Not secret.

Not historical.

Just cables.

Noah sighed.

"This school is becoming very disappointing."

The caretaker opened it during a scheduled inspection.

Inside were wires and one old soda can.

No note.

No key.

No ballot pages.

We photographed it for the repair record and closed it again.

Sophie looked proud of me.

"You didn't even try to climb in."

"I thought about it."

"That's not the same."

"It's progress."

THE DUST DAY

The worst repair day happened during a history test.

At 10:14, a drill started above the classroom.

At 10:15, dust fell onto Noah's paper.

At 10:16, Mr. Vane stopped the test and moved everyone into the library.

Nobody panicked. The caretaker closed the work area. Dr. Rahman inspected the ceiling. The contractor cleaned the room and changed the drilling schedule.

The test continued thirty minutes later.

Noah claimed the dust had erased his best answer.

Mr. Vane gave him a fresh sheet and said the answer could now achieve its full potential elsewhere.

After school, the students involved received a clear explanation and a new test time if they needed it. No rumours. No hidden report. Just an incident, a correction, and a revised timetable.

Sophie added it to the maintenance log. Mia checked that no club had lost its room without warning.

By Friday, the noise complaints had a proper form, a response deadline, and a public summary. It was not dramatic enough for a mystery, which suited everyone.

THE STUDENT WALKTHROUGH

Before the hall reopened, Dr. Rahman invited class representatives to inspect it with her.

She showed them the repaired beams, the new floor, the emergency exits, and the inspection certificates.

Students asked why one yellow support remained near the store cupboard.

It was temporary, unrelated to the roof, and scheduled for removal after the electrical work.

Another question appeared in the public log and received a public answer.

Noah asked whether the new lights could flash during chicken battles.

The electrician demonstrated that they could.

Sophie said they would not.

The walkthrough ended with every representative signing the same form: I KNOW WHERE TO FIND THE SAFETY REPORT.

Not I PROMISE NOTHING WILL EVER BREAK.

That was not a promise anyone could make.

The difference mattered.

THE REPAIR LESSON

Mr. Vane used the repair schedule for a lesson about cause and effect.

Students compared the first alarming report, the new drilling photographs, and Dr. Rahman's final certificate.

The documents did not simply contradict one another. They described different moments and different evidence.

Noah said this explained why his old homework could not be used to prove his new homework was finished.

Mr. Vane said no analogy was perfect.

The class learned to ask who wrote a report, when they wrote it, and what had changed since. Those questions worked for history, buildings, and council decisions.

They also worked for rumours, which was why the next dramatic message about the hall received five requests for a source and no panic.

THE NEW HALL

The repairs finished on schedule.

The crack above the stage was gone.

Gerald had been plastered over.

Noah held a small farewell ceremony.

He read a poem.

"Gerald the crack,
You were at our back.
Then people drilled,
And the hall got filled."

Sophie stopped him before verse two.

Dr. Rahman certified the building safe.

The city removed the closure order.

We could use the hall again.

The first event would be the community council's annual Promise Assembly.

The second would be a joint performance with Westbridge.

The third would be the student-council election.

Sophie put the dates on the board.

Election.

I had forgotten.

My term ended in two weeks.

If I wanted to remain leader, I had to run again.

And this time, my name would not be added secretly.

"TIM STILL RULES."

"I want to run."

The hall became quiet.

Mia had helped with the archive, the homework club, the basketball game, and the community meetings.

She was organised.

Kind.

Brave enough to disagree with adults.

She would be an excellent leader.

My smile disappeared.

Not because she couldn't do it.

Because I suddenly realised other people could.

THE FIRST CAMPAIGN MEETING

Noah wanted to be my campaign manager again.

Sophie said she would remain neutral as council secretary.

Brad said he would support whoever gave him a basketball.

Mia offered him a fair sports budget instead.

He said that sounded like a bribe with receipts.

Noah said this was cruel.

More clubs.

Better lunches.

Longer breaks.

Pizza Fridays.

Then I read it.

It could have been my campaign from last year.

Before the festival.

Before the review.

Before the community council.

Mia's plan was different.

A homework club in every year.

Mental-health quiet times.

Better class-representative training.

Shared events with Westbridge.

It was good.

Very good.

Annoyingly good.

Students told me I had saved the school.

They said I deserved another term.

They promised to vote for me.

It felt good.

Then a Year Seven asked what I would do if the school needed saving again.

"That's not the plan," I said.

"Why not? You're good at mysteries."

Mia was standing nearby.

She didn't say anything.

Chicken battles.

Secret rooms.

Big speeches.

Mia was the person who remembered which students needed help with homework.

"Are you worried?"

"Pizza?"

"Democracy is back."

Recycled paper.

No insults.

No unapproved photographs.

No promises outside the council's budget.

Like someone who understood.

One result.

USEFUL, NOT FAMOUS.

TIM RULES — AND SO DOES EVERYONE ELSE.

Noah said mine was too long.

Sophie said it was accurate.

## Chapter 36: Tim Versus Mia

Tuesday

Running against Mia was much harder than running against Brad.

Brad had called himself stronger, cooler, and better at push-ups.

I knew how to argue with that.

Mia helped younger students with homework and remembered everyone's name.

I could not defeat that with a moustache on her poster.

Noah suggested one anyway.

Sophie banned him from the art cupboard.

My campaign had three promises.

Publish every council decision.

Train class representatives.

Keep the Westbridge partnership active.

Mia's campaign had three promises too.

Make help easier to find.

Make meetings easier to join.

Make sure quiet students were heard.

They sounded similar.

That was because they were both sensible.

Very inconvenient.

THE CAMPAIGN QUESTION BOX

Students submitted anonymous questions.

How will you help students who fall behind?

How will you stop clubs from favouring popular students?

What happens if Westbridge stops cooperating?

Some were not.

"Still."

"Because clarity matters."

Noah wrote another question.

Why do you answer anonymous questions when you know who wrote them?

Mr. Vane gave him extra homework.

Democracy had limits.

THE POPULAR SPEECH

On Wednesday, I tried a new campaign strategy.

Humour.

At lunch, I stood beside my poster and performed the dying-printer noise.

BEEP. BEEP. KRRRR. EEEEE. BZZZZZ.

Students cheered.

Someone shouted, "TIM RULES!"

No audience.

Just help.

Noah looked confused.

"Where's the show?"

"At the homework table."

Twelve students signed up.

Three asked to volunteer.

One student told me my printer noise was better in the first book.

Fair.

Mia and I agreed on one campaign rule ourselves.

Neither of us would use private conversations as campaign material.

If a student asked for help, the conversation would not become a poster quotation without permission.

If one of us made a mistake, the other would not turn it into a rumour.

Noah asked whether embarrassing printer-noise stories counted.

I said public performances were fair game.

Mia said she would not use them anyway.

Sophie wrote the agreement into the campaign guidance for future elections.

The race became less entertaining and much more useful. Students asked questions instead of choosing teams. Brad said he still preferred push-ups, but even he admitted there were fewer arguments at lunch.

Brad offered to endorse me.

"Tim helped save the chess club."

"Vote Tim. He also does small things, but they're harder to remember."

Noah put his head on the table.

I decided to campaign without endorsements.

On Thursday, I forgot a council budget meeting.

I was preparing my debate answers.

Mia attended in my place.

She presented the club requests, took minutes, and published them before I arrived.

Nobody criticised me.

That made it worse.

"Mia followed the process."

"I know."

"Everything is correct."

"I know."

"Sorry I missed the meeting."

I did.

At the next meeting, I read the correction into the record.

No excuses.

No mystery.

Just responsibility.

Students saw it.

Others said it proved I admitted mistakes.

Both were true.

Elections were annoying like that.

THE FINAL DEBATE

"What if we vote for you and you leave for another job?"

Everyone looked at me.

The community-coordinator application was public.

I could not pretend it didn't exist.

"I've applied for the coordinator role too," I said. "If I win council leader, I won't take the other job. If Mia wins, I may apply to support her."

No tricks.
