# TIM RULES 3

## THE OPEN DOOR

*The third Tim Rules book*

## Chapter 1: The Detective Club's First Case

Monday

Being a former student council leader is strange.

People still ask you for advice.

Then they explain why they did not take it.

On the first Monday of the new school year, I found three students outside my old council room.

One held a notebook.

One held a magnifying glass.

One held a packet of biscuits.

The notebook student was called Zara.

The magnifying glass was called Oliver's because she had borrowed it from the chess club.

The biscuits were already being eaten by Noah, who was technically not a student anymore but had returned as a community volunteer.

"Are you Tim?" Zara asked.

"Yes."

"The Tim who solved Room 75?"

"Yes."

"The Tim who found the Festival Cup?"

"Yes."

"The Tim who lost the election to Mia?"

Noah choked on his biscuit.

"Also yes."

Zara opened her notebook.

"We want to reopen the detective club."

Mia had approved it last year.

The club had investigated fence numbers, a missing lunchbox, and a frightening noise in the art cupboard.

The fence numbers were gardening measurements.

The lunchbox was in the owner's bag.

The noise was a radiator.

All excellent cases.

No long-term injuries.

"The club still exists," I said.

Zara shook her head.

"Not officially. Nobody renewed the room booking."

"So your first case is administrative?"

She looked disappointed.

Sophie would have loved it.

THE CASE OF THE MISSING CLUB

We went to see Mia.

She was still council leader, but this year she had a deputy named Eli and two class representatives at every meeting.

Her desk was covered in regional-event forms.

"The detective club didn't submit its continuation form," she said.

Zara opened her mouth.

Mia raised one finger.

Mr. Vane had taught everyone that trick, possibly with Justice Moyo's permission.

"But," Mia continued, "you can submit it now. You need a supervisor, a safety plan, and a project."

Zara pointed at me.

"Tim can supervise."

"I can't. I'm not staff."

"Daniel?"

"He's helping at the bookshop on club days."

"Noah?"

Noah looked up hopefully.

Mrs. Patel, who had entered behind us, said, "No."

Noah lowered his hand.

Mrs. Patel agreed to supervise in the library.

The safety plan was easy.

No investigations alone.

No private accusations.

No entering locked rooms.

No food near archive documents.

Noah objected that the fourth rule made the third one harder because investigations made people hungry.

Mrs. Patel added a fifth rule.

Noah does not choose the evidence-storage locations.

The project was harder.

A club needed a purpose beyond waiting for mysteries.

Ella, now archive coordinator, suggested documenting unsolved questions in the school records.

Not secret clues.

Ordinary gaps.

Missing dates.

Unidentified photographs.

Unlabelled objects.

Questions that could be answered with interviews and research.

Zara liked that.

The club's first case would be a photograph with no names.

THE PHOTOGRAPH

Ella brought out a small black-and-white picture.

It showed twelve students standing beside the oak tree.

The tree was much smaller.

A wooden box sat between them.

On the back was a date.

Exactly twenty-five years ago next month.

Under the date, someone had written:

PROMISE CAPSULE — OPEN WHEN THE NEXT COUNCIL IS READY.

Noah leaned over my shoulder.

"Time capsule."

I tried not to look excited.

I failed.

Zara tried too.

She failed worse.

Ella smiled.

"The box in the photograph is not in our inventory."

"Where is it?" Zara asked.

"Unknown."

"Who buried it?"

"Unknown."

"Who are the students?"

"That is your first research question."

Noah looked at the tree.

"Can we dig?"

"No," everyone said.

Zara wrote three tasks in her notebook.

Identify students.

Find capsule records.

Ask whether next council is ready.

Mia looked at the regional forms on her desk.

"The council is about to be very busy. The city has invited Brookfield and Westbridge to host the first Regional Student Charter Festival. Schools from across the area will send representatives."

"What is a student charter?" Zara asked.

"A set of promises written by students from different schools," Mia said. "How decisions should work. How records should be kept. How students should be included."

Ella pointed to the photograph.

"A capsule from twenty-five years ago could be part of the opening ceremony."

There it was.

A real historical project.

No forged signatures.

No threatened school.

No criminal hiding in a van.

Just a box, twelve students, and a question about whether we were ready.

THE FIRST INTERVIEW

The easiest person to identify was the teacher in the corner.

Mr. Vane.

Younger.

Same eyebrows.

He looked at the photograph during his free period.

"Promise Club," he said.

"Were you a member?" Zara asked.

"No. I was a trainee teacher. I thought the club was inefficient."

Noah nodded.

"That sounds like your review period."

Mr. Vane ignored him.

"The students came from Brookfield, Westbridge, and two schools that later closed. They wanted a regional student council."

"What happened?"

"The city rejected the proposal. Adults decided students could not manage shared decisions."

"Did they prove otherwise?"

"Not then. They argued. Their schools competed. The project ended before the capsule was opened."

Zara looked at Mia's regional forms.

History was giving us a second attempt.

"Who wrote the charter?" I asked.

"A student named Samira Bell."

Clara's cousin.

Another Bell.

Another bookshop visit.

Mr. Vane handed the photograph back.

"Before you ask, I do not know where the capsule is. I only remember that Samira refused to bury it until every school had signed the charter."

"Did every school sign?"

"No."

Then why did the photograph say open when the next council was ready?

Perhaps the capsule was never buried.

Perhaps it was still waiting.

THE NOTE IN THE PHOTOGRAPH FRAME

Ella removed the photograph from its old frame so we could scan the back properly.

A second piece of paper slipped out.

It was new.

White paper.

Black ink.

A folded-wing hawk drawn at the top.

Zara gasped.

Noah whispered, "Here we go."

The message said:

THE FIRST CHARTER FAILED.

THE NEXT ONE WILL TOO.

FIND THE PROMISE THAT WAS NEVER COUNTED.

Underneath was the number ten.

Not nine.

Ten.

For two books, nine had been the important number.

Nine promises.

Nine drawers.

Nine original class representatives.

Now someone had written ten.

I looked at Zara.

She was already writing questions.

Good.

This was her club.

Her case.

My job was not to take over.

"Where do we start?" she asked me.

I pointed to the photograph.

"Names before mysteries."

She nodded.

"Interviews before digging?"

"Yes."

"Forms before accusations?"

"Sophie has trained you well."

Zara smiled.

The detective club had its first official case.

And for once, I was only an adviser.

Mostly.

## Chapter 2: Samira's List

Tuesday

Clara knew every name in the photograph.

She also knew everyone had bad hair.

"That was the style," she said.

Noah studied his own hair.

"Will people say that about me?"

"Yes."

Clara spread the picture beneath the bookshop lamp.

Samira Bell stood in the centre.

Lucas's mother stood beside her.

Justice Moyo's older cousin stood at the end.

The Westbridge representative was a boy named Martin Doyle.

Mrs. Doyle's older brother.

Every family seemed connected to the school records somehow.

Zara wrote all twelve names.

There were three Brookfield students, three Westbridge students, three from North Park, and three from Riverside.

The last two schools had changed names since then.

The regional charter had involved the same neighbourhoods but not the same buildings.

"What was the promise that wasn't counted?" Zara asked.

Clara shook her head.

"Samira called it the absent vote."

Noah frowned.

"People who didn't vote?"

"People who weren't in the room."

Clara pulled a box of old programmes from beneath the counter.

"The first regional meeting was invitation-only. Each school sent its most confident students. Samira objected. She said a charter for everyone could not be written only by students adults already liked."

Ella smiled.

That sounded like her quiet-room rule.

Mia's rule too.

Ask who is missing.

"Did Samira win?"

"No. The meeting continued. She left and took the unsigned charter."

"So there was no tenth promise," I said.

"There may have been. Samira wrote about an additional principle, but the official record only includes nine."

Zara underlined the phrase absent vote.

The note had not told us to find treasure.

It had told us to find missing people.

THE LIST OF THE ABSENT

Samira had kept a list of students excluded from the first meeting.

Not names.

Reasons.

Too young.

Too quiet.

Behind in class.

New to the school.

Poor attendance.

Not elected.

Not interested.

Noah stopped at the last one.

"If they're not interested, isn't that their choice?"

"Yes," Clara said. "Samira wrote that choice was different from exclusion. Students could refuse to participate. They should first receive a real invitation."

Zara copied the list.

Mia joined us after school and read it quietly.

The regional festival invitation had come from the city.

Each school would send five representatives.

Selected by the school.

Exactly like twenty-five years ago.

"We're doing it again," she said.

Nobody answered.

Mia turned the city letter over.

"We need open nominations. Any student can apply. Representatives chosen by their classes. Places reserved for clubs and community groups. Remote participation for students who can't attend."

Ella nodded.

"And publish the selection process."

Zara added one more.

"Ask students who don't apply why not."

Noah raised his hand.

"And snacks for people attending online."

"That one may be difficult."

THE FIRST PUBLIC OBJECTION

At the next council meeting, Mia proposed the open-selection plan.

Mr. Vane supported it.

Sophie asked how much it would cost.

The principal asked whether the city would accept additional representatives.

Ms. Dube, now regional education coordinator, said yes within the venue capacity.

The capacity was fifty students.

Ten schools.

Five each.

I could not invite everyone physically.

The solution was a linked festival.

Local meetings at every school.

Shared online sessions.

Representatives rotating between events.

Every student could participate somewhere.

No one building had to hold the entire region.

The council approved the plan.

Westbridge agreed.

Lucas had graduated too, but he remained a youth partner like Noah and me.

The current Westbridge leader was a student named Priya.

She liked systems almost as much as Sophie and basketball almost as much as Brad.

This was either perfect or terrifying.

THE CLEARPATH OFFER

The city offered free festival software from a company called ClearPath Learning.

It would manage nominations, votes, documents, translations, and public records.

A representative named Mara Flint attended by video.

She had a friendly voice and a presentation full of colourful arrows.

"ClearPath removes administrative barriers," she said. "One secure platform. One verified student identity. One reliable record."

Sophie liked the audit tools.

Ella liked the translation support.

Mia liked the remote access.

I disliked the phrase one verified student identity.

Not because verification was bad.

Because some students did not have city email accounts or stable internet.

Mara answered that printed codes would be available.

Every objection had a feature.

That should have been reassuring.

Instead, I wondered whether the software had been designed before anyone asked the students.

Mr. Vane asked who owned the records.

"Schools retain ownership," Mara said.

"Who can access them?"

"Authorised staff and contracted support teams."

"Can students export everything?"

"At the end of the programme."

Sophie asked, "Why not continuously?"

Mara smiled.

"Continuous export creates security risks."

Justice Moyo, attending as an advisor, asked for an independent privacy review before approval.

Mara agreed.

The decision was delayed.

Good.

Public process working.

THE FIRST ALTERED RECORD

That evening, a screenshot appeared online.

It showed Brookfield's internal nomination form.

A line at the bottom said:

Only students with attendance above 95% may participate.

No such rule had been approved.

Students became angry.

Parents called the office.

Mia checked the original form.

No attendance condition.

The screenshot was fake.

But it looked exactly like the new ClearPath template.

We had not started using ClearPath.

Mara said the screenshot must be an old demonstration page.

Justice asked how a student had obtained it.

Mara could not explain.

Zara opened her detective notebook.

"First altered record. First public suspect."

I shook my head.

"First altered record. No suspect yet."

She nodded and wrote:

Who benefits if students reject the festival software?

Who benefits if students demand the city provide it?

Two different lists.

Good detective work.

At the bottom, she added:

Why did the fake rule target attendance?

I remembered Samira's absent list.

Poor attendance.

New students.

Too quiet.

The same exclusions returning in a new form.

Whoever made the screenshot knew the old charter.

Or knew exactly how to frighten a school that had spent two years fighting to be heard.

## Chapter 3: The Promise Capsule

Wednesday

We found the capsule record in a school newsletter.

Not the capsule.

The record.

Zara said historical research involved too many sentences beginning with "not the."

The newsletter showed Samira and Martin beside a metal box.

The headline said:

STUDENTS SEAL REGIONAL PROMISES FOR THE FUTURE.

The article listed the contents.

Unsigned charter.

Student letters.

A recorded debate.

One object from each school.

A "record of those not present."

And a key held by the regional council.

There was no regional council now.

There was a city education department, a youth forum, and Ms. Dube's calendar with no free space until Thursday.

The burial location was described only as:

Between the roots of cooperation.

Noah looked at the photograph of the oak.

"Tree."

Zara shook her head.

"Too obvious."

Brad, who had come to return a basketball, said, "Under the gym?"

Also too obvious.

Sophie read the sentence twice.

"Between roots could mean a foundation. The capsule might be in the boundary between school sites."

Now that was less obvious.

THE FOUR SCHOOL MAP

The original four schools had shared a community centre.

Each building stood around a central garden.

The garden was gone.

A road crossed it.

In the middle was a roundabout with a stone planter.

Noah looked at the map.

"We are not digging up a roundabout."

"No," Zara said.

He seemed genuinely disappointed.

Ms. Dube arranged access to city archives instead.

The garden construction plans showed a foundation stone donated by students.

A metal cavity had been built beneath the planter.

The cavity was still there.

The city had inspected it during road work and resealed it.

No record of removing anything.

The capsule might be inside.

On public property.

Under flowers.

Beside moving traffic.

This was why adults invented supervised site visits.

THE OPENING PERMISSION

Justice arranged for the capsule to be opened during a public history event at the community centre.

No students near the road.

A city work crew lifted the planter.

A metal lid appeared underneath.

It had four school crests.

Brookfield.

Westbridge.

North Park.

Riverside.

Zara stood behind the barrier with her notebook.

Mia stood beside her.

Ella carried archive gloves.

Noah carried emergency biscuits and was told not to eat them near the cavity.

Mr. Vane watched the work crew with his arms folded.

He was probably remembering the original students.

Clara stood with Samira Bell.

Samira had arrived by train.

She was older than Clara, with short white hair and a walking stick decorated with small hawk stickers.

"You found my photograph," she said to Zara.

"Yes."

"Did you find my absent list?"

"Yes."

"Good. The list mattered more than the box."

The work crew removed the lid.

Inside was a metal capsule.

Exactly like the newsletter.

No false bottom.

No missing box.

Just twenty-five years of waiting.

THE CAPSULE CONTENTS

Samira opened it at the community-centre table.

The seal broke with a soft crack.

Inside were envelopes, a film reel, a cassette, a wooden hawk, and four school badges.

Everything had been wrapped in waxed cloth.

Most of it was dry.

The letters had faded but could be read.

The unsigned charter had nine promises.

At the bottom, in Samira's handwriting, was a tenth line.

No decision about us without a way for us to answer.

Not no decision without us.

A way to answer.

More practical.

Students might not attend every meeting.

They might not vote on every issue.

But they needed a way to respond.

Zara read it aloud.

Mia looked at the regional nomination plan.

Online sessions.

Local meetings.

Open comments.

Public corrections.

All ways to answer.

"The tenth promise wasn't uncounted," Ella said. "It was unapproved."

Samira nodded.

"The adults said nine looked tidier."

Noah sighed.

"Adults love tidy numbers."

Mr. Vane looked at him.

"So do students when the number is their score."

Fair.

THE RECORDED DEBATE

The cassette worked in Daniel's repaired player.

Young voices filled the room.

Students argued about representation.

Some wanted elected councils only.

Some wanted open meetings.

One said quiet students should not be forced to speak.

Another said confident students should stop speaking for them.

Samira's voice came last.

"We cannot promise everyone will agree. We can promise everyone will know how to disagree."

The recording ended with laughter and a chair scraping.

No dramatic clue.

No hidden accusation.

Just students having the same argument twenty-five years earlier.

Then the tape continued.

A second voice, much quieter, read a list of names.

Students not present.

For each name, a sentence.

"Ruth wanted to join but had no transport."

"Omar was told his attendance was too low."

"Evelyn was new and had no class representative."

"Michael said meetings made him anxious."

The absent list had names after all.

Samira closed her eyes.

"I recorded that after everyone left. I didn't want their reasons reduced to categories."

Zara held her notebook still.

She had stopped writing.

Listening came first.

THE FILM REEL

The film reel needed specialist equipment.

The public library offered to digitise it.

Mrs. Patel said the process would take one week.

We placed every item in labelled archive boxes.

Samira insisted the tenth promise be displayed immediately.

Mia wanted to include it in the regional festival.

Ms. Dube agreed to put it on the draft agenda.

Within hours, a new photograph appeared online.

It showed the tenth line.

Below it was a caption:

BROOKFIELD INVENTS HISTORY TO CONTROL REGIONAL CHARTER.

The post claimed Samira had written the line recently.

The ink was new, it said.

The capsule was a stunt.

The attached image looked convincing.

The faded line had been digitally darkened while the rest looked old.

Students from other schools shared it.

The capsule had been open for less than three hours.

Only a small group knew the exact wording.

Zara looked around the community centre.

Family members.

City staff.

Students.

Archive volunteers.

Mara Flint's ClearPath camera crew recording the event for promotional material.

One of them had posted quickly.

Or someone had photographed someone else's notes.

Ella checked the public archive upload.

The tenth-promise scan had been replaced by the altered version.

Her account had uploaded it.

Again.

Another trusted account.

Another public lie.

This time, the detective club had a case big enough for the whole region.

## Chapter 4: The Altered Archive

Thursday

Ella did not panic.

That was how I knew things were serious.

She photographed the altered upload, exported the activity log, and locked her account.

Then she called an archive meeting in public.

Not a secret investigation.

A public correction.

The original tenth-promise scan remained on her offline backup.

The archive's version history showed two uploads.

The first at 4:12 p.m.

The second at 4:19 p.m.

Seven minutes apart.

The altered version had been uploaded from the community-centre Wi-Fi.

During the capsule event.

Using Ella's session on her own laptop.

"Did you leave it unlocked?" Sophie asked.

Ella nodded.

"For two minutes. I helped Samira with the cassette player."

No secret hacking.

No master password.

Just an open laptop.

Noah looked at his own bag.

"I've left mine unlocked everywhere."

"Yes," Sophie said.

The problem was not a master criminal.

It was a system that made one mistake enough to change public history.

THE PUBLIC CORRECTION

Mia posted both versions side by side.

Original scan.

Altered upload.

Activity log.

Explanation.

No accusation.

The archive added a visible banner:

THIS RECORD WAS ALTERED WITHOUT AUTHORISATION. CORRECTION IN PROGRESS.

People shared the correction.

Not as quickly as the lie.

But enough.

Samira gave a statement confirming the handwriting and the capsule seal.

Mrs. Patel confirmed the original scan's time.

The city preservation officer confirmed the paper age could be tested if necessary.

There was no need.

The version history proved the alteration.

Zara asked whether I should remove the altered copy.

Ella said no.

"Keep it labelled. It is evidence of what happened."

Public history included the attack on public history.

Good.

THE CAMERA CREW

ClearPath had filmed the capsule opening.

Mara Flint offered the footage voluntarily.

The camera showed Ella leaving her laptop.

At 4:17, a student in a Brookfield hoodie stood beside it.

Face hidden.

At 4:18, the screen changed.

At 4:19, the student walked away.

Height average.

Shoes ordinary.

No identifying features.

Noah said the hoodie was suspicious.

Zara said half of Brookfield owned one.

She was right.

The camera followed the student for three seconds before losing them behind the crowd.

In the reflection of the window, a small badge flashed.

Not Brookfield.

Westbridge.

Priya watched the footage.

"We gave visitor badges to twelve Westbridge students."

"How many returned them?" Sophie asked.

"Eleven."

One missing badge.

The visitor log showed which student had not returned it.

Priya's deputy, Aaron.

Aaron said he had lost the badge before the event.

He had reported it at reception.

Reception confirmed.

Someone else could have found it.

Another too-obvious suspect.

I was tired of too-obvious suspects.

Zara wrote:

Badge proves someone had a badge. Nothing else.

Good.

THE MISSING BADGE

Reception found the badge in a returned archive box.

The box had come from North Park.

North Park's coordinator said their students never used Westbridge badges.

The box had passed through ClearPath's transport crew.

Mara said her staff had followed procedures.

The driver said the box was already open.

Mrs. Patel said it had been sealed.

Three accounts.

One missing seal.

Zara wanted to interview everyone.

Justice allowed the club to ask questions only with staff permission and adult supervision.

No secret meetings.

No accusations online.

No investigating students' private accounts.

Zara agreed.

Then she asked me whether I thought Aaron was guilty.

"I don't know."

"But you suspect someone."

"I suspect the person wanted us to suspect Aaron."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only evidence-based one."

She sighed.

"Being responsible makes investigations slower."

"Yes."

"Is it more fun?"

"No."

She laughed.

At least I was honest.

THE PLATFORM VOTE

The archive alteration made some schools reject ClearPath.

Others demanded ClearPath immediately because they believed a single secure platform would stop fake posts.

Mara's presentation changed overnight.

Now every slide mentioned security.

Protect student voices.

Prevent archive manipulation.

Verify every contribution.

Her company benefited from the fear whether schools wanted the platform or feared not having it.

That did not make her guilty.

It made the decision important.

Mia proposed a two-week pilot with strict conditions.

No official votes on the platform.

No private student data beyond names and school roles.

Daily exports.

Independent privacy review.

Public logs.

Paper alternatives.

ClearPath accepted every condition except daily exports.

"System architecture does not support it," Mara said.

Sophie replied, "Then the architecture does not support our public-records policy."

The room became quiet.

Mara asked for one day to consult her engineers.

Mr. Vane leaned toward me.

"That was a satisfactory answer."

"Sophie?"

"Yes."

I agreed.

THE SECOND ACCOUNT

That night, a new account called Ninth Hawk posted a list of Brookfield students who supposedly supported the attendance rule.

Names.

Classes.

Private club memberships.

All presented as evidence of exclusion.

Some details were wrong.

Some were accurate enough to be frightening.

The accurate details came from the festival nomination test form.

The form had been stored on Ella's laptop.

Not in the public archive.

Whoever altered the scan had copied student information too.

This was no longer only history vandalism.

It was a privacy breach.

Justice closed the festival nomination system temporarily.

Mia cancelled online sign-ups.

The regional charter preparation stopped.

And Ninth Hawk posted one new sentence.

PAPER RECORDS CANNOT PROTECT YOU EITHER.

Attached was a photograph of the council-room key cabinet.

Taken from inside the school.

## Chapter 5: The Key Cabinet

Friday

The key cabinet was locked.

The keys were inside.

Nothing was missing.

The photograph showed the cabinet open at 6:42 the previous evening.

The school had been open for a drama rehearsal.

Forty students.

Eight adults.

One caretaker.

Any of them could have passed the office.

The cabinet log said it had been opened by Sophie.

Sophie said she had returned the archive key at 6:40.

She had locked it immediately.

The photograph timestamp could be wrong.

Or the cabinet had been reopened.

Or the picture had been taken earlier and posted with a new time.

Zara examined the image.

A reflection in the metal door showed the photographer's hand.

A black sleeve.

A silver ring.

No face.

No student badge.

No useful detail.

"Could be an adult," she said.

"Could be anyone with a ring," I replied.

She sighed.

"Evidence is very picky."

PAPER RECORDS

Ninth Hawk's threat about paper records forced us to check the archive.

Not secretly.

With Mrs. Patel and the office manager.

Every shelf.

Every box.

Every key.

The original documents were safe.

But one register of key loans had a page removed.

The page covered the capsule event.

Without it, we could not prove who had borrowed which keys.

Ella photographed the rough paper edge.

The page had been cut neatly, not torn.

A small blade.

Time.

Access.

This was not a student grabbing an open laptop for seven minutes.

Someone had planned a second breach.

Mia closed the council room until the locks could be changed.

Justice notified the city.

The principal told students there was no immediate safety threat but personal information might have been accessed.

Parents demanded details.

The school published what it knew and what it did not.

No hiding.

No invented reassurance.

THE PAPER BACKUP

Sophie had a backup key log.

Of course.

A handwritten duplicate stored with the caretaker.

It showed every key loan from the capsule day.

At 6:45, after Sophie returned the archive key, the caretaker had opened the cabinet for Mara Flint.

Mara said she needed to check the festival storage room for ClearPath equipment.

The caretaker had escorted her there.

He had not watched her while she used her phone near the office.

Mara admitted taking a photograph of the cabinet.

She said it was for a security assessment.

"Why post it through Ninth Hawk?" Zara asked during the supervised interview.

"I did not post it."

"Who did you send it to?"

"My security team."

"Can you show the message?"

Mara handed over her phone.

The photograph had been sent at 6:44 to a ClearPath group chat.

Four employees had access.

One was the driver connected to the archive box.

One was the camera operator who had filmed Ella's laptop.

One was a software engineer named Ren.

One was Mara.

All had means.

All had reasons to assess security.

None had a known reason to expose student information publicly.

Mara's expression remained calm.

"ClearPath would never benefit from this breach."

Sophie looked at her.

"Your company is being asked to solve it."

"Because schools deserve secure systems."

"Or because insecurity is good marketing?"

Mara ended the interview.

Justice did not stop her.

She had answered voluntarily and could leave.

Zara wrote four names in her notebook.

Then she wrote POSSIBLE, NOT PROVEN underneath.

Good.

THE PRIVACY MEETING

The regional schools held an emergency public meeting.

Students wanted to know what information had been copied.

Names.

Classes.

Club memberships.

Nomination interests.

No home addresses.

No medical records.

No financial details.

That mattered.

The harm was real but not unlimited.

Mia apologised for the open laptop even though Ella had been the one using it.

Ella apologised too.

Sophie explained the new controls.

Two-person archive access.

Automatic screen locks.

Offline backups.

No nomination data on personal devices.

Paper forms available.

Mara offered ClearPath's secure system free for a year.

This time, several schools supported accepting immediately.

Fear had changed the vote.

Justice proposed waiting until the independent privacy review.

North Park refused.

They wanted an immediate platform.

Westbridge supported the pilot conditions.

Riverside wanted paper only.

Brookfield proposed a mixed system.

The regional charter had not been written and schools were already arguing about who controlled the record.

Exactly like twenty-five years ago.

SAMIRA'S WARNING

After the meeting, Samira asked Zara and me to sit with her beneath the oak.

"You think this is about software," she said.

"Isn't it?" Zara asked.

"Partly. Mostly it is about who gets to say a student is legitimate."

When the playground vote began, she used the response form rather than speaking. The facilitator collected it with the others and counted her choice exactly once.

No one announced that the system had worked. It simply had.

THE COUNCIL ROOM

The council room door was open.

Not because someone had forgotten to close it.

Because the meeting was public and the room was accessible.

A sign showed the next private agenda item, its legal reason, and when the public session would resume.

Transparency with boundaries.

Inside, students discussed playground repairs.

No hidden vote.

No dramatic deadline.

Just costs, accessibility, and three possible designs.

I stood at the back until someone noticed me.

"Tim!"

A few students turned.

The chair asked whether I wanted to observe or contribute.

"Observe."

I took a seat.

They continued.

Perfect.

ROOM 75

Later, I passed Room 75.

The glass door showed a class using the repaired first-council table.

Students compared two accounts of a sports match.

One drawer contained historical chalk.

One held consent cards.

One held archived keys.

None held a secret future.

The timeline's blank FUTURE HISTORY section had been replaced several times.

Current students wrote and revised their own events.

The old heavy door stood in the archive workshop as an object students could study without being locked behind it.

Noah would have found the whole thing disappointingly safe.

Excellent.

THE OAK TREE

The oak had grown.

The stone marker remained legible with occasional conservation.

WHAT WE PROMISE, WE BECOME.

Around it, younger trees marked classes and community groups.

Some promises tied years earlier had been preserved digitally when paper faded.

No one pretended physical objects lasted forever.

The archive preserved meaning while gardeners preserved roots.

Different responsibilities.

Shared care.

Ruth's bus stopped nearby.

Students stepped off carrying meeting notes and sports bags.

Michael's written-response group met beneath the tree on dry days.

Brad's basketball team trained on the court beyond it.

The place held more stories than any one book could contain.

THE FINAL DETECTIVE-CLUB CASE

Zara caught up with me beside the field.

"One last case?"

"Which one?"

"Someone left a folded hawk note in the community diary."

My stomach jumped out of habit.

"What did it say?"

She handed me a copy.

COME TO THE BOOKSHOP AT FOUR. PUBLIC EVENT. EVERYONE INVITED.

Signed:

Amara, June, Tobi, Eli, and twenty students.

No anonymous clue.

No private meeting.

No fabricated mystery.

"That's not a case," I said.

"No. It's an invitation."

Better.

THE BOOKSHOP AT FOUR

The bookshop was full.

Students had organised a public launch for the next community-history guide.

Not my guide.

Theirs.

Interviews.

Maps.

Corrections.

Fictional training mysteries clearly labelled.

A chapter about why not every locked door needs opening.

A chapter about what to do when adults disagree.

A chapter about records that remain incomplete.

A recipe appendix with allergy-safe emergency biscuits.

Noah had contributed after all.

Amara asked me to say one sentence.

Only one.

They knew me.

I looked at the open door, the students, the old Black Hawks, the new councils, and the friends who had grown beyond every role I remembered.

"Leave room for the people who will tell this story differently."

Then I sat down.

They continued without me.

THE LAST PAGE

That evening, I wrote in my own notebook for the first time in months.

Three books ago, I thought Tim Rules meant being in charge.

Two books ago, I learned everyone rules.

One book ago, I learned to leave the door open.

Now I understood that an open door was not the ending.

People still had to walk through, argue, repair things, correct the record, and make room for whoever came next.

The work continued.

So did the jokes.

Noah sent a photograph of Train the rubber chicken sitting in my old council chair.

Sophie sent a correction saying the chair was not assigned to poultry.

Brad sent a basketball score.

Mia sent a new policy article.

Zara sent the guide's public link.

Mr. Vane sent one underlined sentence about historical humility.

I closed the notebook.

Outside, someone knocked.

Not a mysterious note.

Just Mum asking whether I wanted dinner.

"Coming."

I left my room and held the door open for her.

She carried food, not evidence.

We went downstairs together.

And honestly...

That was enough.
